An open-plan office does not get loud through ill will; it gets loud through hard materials and too many people trying to be understood at the same time. **Improving the acoustics almost always comes down to a combination of three things: adding absorption (ceiling and walls), screening zones (partitions and booths) and agreeing where calls and meetings happen.** Below are the seven measures we at Re-Sound see working in practice, in the order in which they pay back most.

## Why open-plan offices are so loud

Concrete, glass, plasterboard and resin floors absorb hardly any sound. Every voice bounces around the room several times before it dies away: that is reverberation. The longer the reverberation, the louder the background level, and the louder everyone speaks to be heard above it. That bidding war is the Lombard effect, and it explains why an office at three in the afternoon is so much louder than at eight in the morning.

The second problem is speech intelligibility: not the volume, but the fact that you *understand* your colleagues. A conversation you can follow pulls at your attention whether you want it to or not. For open-plan offices ISO 22955 (2021) therefore describes not only reverberation but above all how quickly speech falls away with distance (measured to ISO 3382-3). A good open-plan office is one where you can no longer make out your colleagues at six metres. For UK projects BS 8233 is the general pointer on sound in buildings; check the exact requirement with your acoustician.

## Measure first: reverberation time and speech decay

Before you buy anything, measure. A reverberation time measurement (RT60, to ISO 3382-2) takes half a day and gives you, per octave band, the figure you can calculate with. The guide values we work to:

- **Open-plan floor**: 0.5–0.8 s, depending on ceiling height and the number of workstations.
- **Meeting rooms**: ≤ 0.6 s.
- **Focus zones and booths**: as low as possible, ≤ 0.5 s.

If you measure 1.0 s or more, absorption is the first step. If you are already around 0.6 s and it is still restless, speech decay over distance is the problem, and zoning and booths help more than extra panels. Re-Sound measures on request in the [showroom](/en/where-to-buy) or at your premises.

## 1. Start with the ceiling

The ceiling is the largest continuous surface and the only one every workstation can see. An absorbent ceiling brings the reverberation time down fastest. Two options:

- **An acoustic stretch ceiling**: a perforated membrane with absorbent material behind it, seamless up to 5 m wide, fitted in a day. Our sister company STRETCH has been installing them in offices for years; details on [its own site](https://stretchplafond.be/products/acoustic-stretch-system).
- **Ceiling panels and rafts** in recycled PET or wood, where the ceiling has to stay visible (services on show, concrete as a finish).

Rule of thumb: making 60 to 80% of the ceiling area absorbent solves the reverberation problem in most offices.

## 2. Wall panels on the reflection points

Walls come after the ceiling, but not just anywhere. Sound reflects hardest off the walls facing the source and off the long walls of a rectangular room. That is where the panels belong:

- **[rPET Groove](/en/products/rpet-groove)** (grooved, ten stock colours and any colour to order, 12/24/36 mm) for large wall areas that may carry colour. NRC 0.55 / 0.75 / 0.90 according to thickness; fire class B-s1,d0 in white, grey and black, B-s2,d0 in the other colours.
- **[rWood Groove](/en/products/rwood-groove) or rWood Micro** where the interior asks for warmth: FSC®-certified veneer on an MDF core (Groove has MDF lamellas on 3 mm felt of recycled PET, Micro a fire-retardant MDF core with sound chambers), αw 0.90 (class A).
- **[Interior](/en/products/interior)**, our modular textile panels with a removable, washable cover, for zones where panels are often swapped or moved.

Hang panels at ear height up to about 2.2 m: that is where speech reflects. A panel above the cupboards does far less than the same panel behind a desk.

## 3. Zone with acoustic partitions

An open-plan office of forty workstations is acoustically one room. ## 5. Meeting pods instead of building more meeting rooms

Whoever has too few meeting rooms sees meetings land on the floor. A **[Modular XL](/en/products/modular-xl)** pod seats six to ten people, grows in 90 cm segments, and stands there without planning permission or building work. **[Duo](/en/products/duo)** catches the small huddles: two seats for a one-to-one, or one desk across the full width for half a day of focused work.

## 6. Soft materials and furniture

Carpet tiles, upholstered chairs, curtains and bookcases each absorb a little. Carpet instead of a resin floor alone makes a noticeable difference in a large office to the impact noise of footsteps and chairs. Do not count these measures as your main absorption (a carpet does not reach αw 0.90), but take them along in every refit.

## 7. Agreements and layout

The cheapest measure: seat the callers together, and not next to the people who concentrate all day. Do not route walkways and the coffee point straight through the work zones. Agree that calls go in the booth and that group discussions move to the pod. Without those agreements, people fill the silence the panels create straight back up.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a good reverberation time for an open-plan office?**
Between 0.5 and 0.8 seconds, depending on ceiling height and occupancy. Above 1 second, absorption (ceiling, walls) is the first step; at around 0.6 seconds and still restless, zoning and phone booths help more.

**Do acoustic panels help against the noise of colleagues?**
Yes, in two ways: they shorten the reverberation so the background level drops, and they reduce the distance at which you can still understand your colleagues. They do not make a conversation on the floor inaudible; that is what phone booths and pods are for.

**How many panels does an open-plan office need?**
As a rule of thumb, 15 to 20% of the floor area in class A or B absorption, on top of an absorbent ceiling. Our guide on how many acoustic panels you need works it out for four rooms.
